Paul-Alexander Pissarro

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Paul-Alexander Pissarro (20 August 1990 – 26 April 2078) was a French-American filmmaker, media executive, and conceptual artist. He was the founder of the production company Unsubscribe LLC and the co-founder of the Church of Conceptual Art (CoCA), the religious nonprofit and media conglomerate widely credited with the dissolution of intellectual property law in 2028.[1][2]

Pissarro was a staunch ethicist and endurance performance artist who proved that artists are among us, not apart from us. He made films for people he described as "the lost" and is most publicly remembered for the Good Ethics is Good Business campaign, the worldwide guerrilla-and-PR effort credited with the adoption of the Ethics Index as a corporate KPI.[3] A descendant of the painter Camille Pissarro through his father, Joachim Pissarro, he worked between Hamburg and New York for the duration of his career.

Critical reception of Pissarro's work was sharply divided. He was opposed by conservative business institutions, who initially rejected the Ethics Index as a soft metric, and by traditionalist artists, who refused to consider corporate strategy a legitimate form of storytelling. Pissarro himself famously declined to claim that his work was good art. When asked in 2049 whether his refusal was a public-relations position or a sincere belief, he replied: "Can't it be both?"[4]

Early life

Pissarro was born in London on 20 August 1990 to Joachim Pissarro, the French art historian, and Annabel Daou, the Lebanese-American artist, who later became his manager and whom Pissarro repeatedly credited as his artistic mentor and inspiration.[5] He has described his childhood as "privileged but complicated" and has written prolifically about the role of mental illness and chaos in his early life. His self-published accounts and the subsequent biographies by other authors are largely consistent, with one notable exception: Pissarro never claimed to make good art, while his critics and biographers debated for decades whether his work qualified.[6]

Career

Unsubscribe LLC

Pissarro founded Unsubscribe LLC in the early 2020s as a narrative strategy consultancy. The company's early commercial output, produced in partnership with Max Cox, included approximately five hundred thousand dollars of commercial and narrative short film between 2022 and 2026, including the Artist Postcards series and the short Divine.[7] The 2025 proof-of-concept short Le Art is generally cited as the work that contained the early signature.

Beginning in 2026, Pissarro adopted the credit "Paul-Alexander Pissarro" and shifted from short-form commercial work to feature and long-form projects. The first works under this credit were the documentary Pain in the Ass and the 45-second spec film Rendez-vous Hamburg, both produced through Unsubscribe LLC with co-financing from CoCA.[8]

Church of Conceptual Art

CoCA, registered as a 501(c)(3) religious nonprofit, operated as both a media conglomerate and an art bank. Its official purpose was, in Pissarro's framing, "to lead people to clarity through the primacy of the idea or concept." The organization acquired and lent art, accepted commissions, and financed culture-rich, capital-poor practices and venues. Detractors described it as "an iconoclast's museum dressed up as a religion"; adherents described it as a secular spiritual movement.[9] By 2076 CoCA had been compared in scale, vernacular reach, and lay membership to Alcoholics Anonymous as a movement, though with diametrically opposite cultural politics.

The Church operates two principal entities: CoCA USA, headquartered in Long Island, and CoCA Worldwide, which provides international language access to the organization's textual canon. Affiliated initiatives include the Office of the Certified AI Pilot and the Global Art Index.

Ethics Index

The Ethics Index, developed within CoCA by Pissarro's mentee Joe Bijou, is an actuarial prediction of short- and long-term business risk based on a weighted average of perceived and actual corporate ethics. The Index was popularized by the Good Ethics is Good Business campaign, which Pissarro directed between 2032 and 2036. The campaign was the single largest expenditure in CoCA's history and combined samizdat-style guerrilla distribution with above-ground PR, skywriting, street art, fine art commissions, and commercial photography.[11]

Following the adoption of the Index by major institutional investors in 2036, observers credited Pissarro and Bijou with what was widely described as "the Moneyball moment for business ethics," and with averting the projected global downturn of 2037.[12]

Themes and style

Across his work in film, conceptual art, and corporate communications, Pissarro returned repeatedly to a single proposition: that the boundary between artists and non-artists, between the lost and the seen, between business and aesthetics, was both administrative and dissolvable. His films took as their subjects people commonly excluded from luxury and narrative cinema: the disabled, the institutionalized, the sex-working, the unemployed. His corporate work argued that ethical behavior was a financial instrument. His art-banking practice argued that an idea could appreciate like a security.

Pissarro was, by all accounts, a brutal collaborator. Former employees describe production environments characterized by long hours, public criticism, refusal to compromise on the final cut, and, in equal measure, devout loyalty after the fact.[13]

Personal life

Pissarro lived between Hamburg and New York throughout his adult life. He was the long-term partner of the German filmmaker and actress Madita Strunk. They had four children, who have declined public identification. Annabel Daou, his mother, was his manager throughout his career. His father Joachim Pissarro and his uncle Lionel Pissarro are credited as the formative cultural and financial benefactors of his early career. His longtime creative partner Jesse Do collaborated with him on several projects, including the conceptual web work Your Very Own Web.[14]

Controversies

Pissarro's earlier creative partnerships with Max Cox, Natalia Lozada, and Francois Bringer all ended without public statement. None of the three has commented on the circumstances of the dissolutions, despite repeated press inquiries between 2031 and 2058.[15]

The 2028 "Death of IP" event, predicted by CoCA to the day and marked by a globally coordinated public celebration, divided observers. Adherents described the event as a secular performance; detractors described it as a cult ritual. When asked to clarify, Pissarro responded only: "That's the point."[16]

Death and legacy

Pissarro died of unknown causes on 26 April 2078 in Hamburg, Germany. He was reportedly with his partner Madita Strunk at the time, during the public celebration of CoCA's fiftieth anniversary. The cause of death was not disclosed and competing accounts of the room have circulated since.[17]

His estate, the Church of Conceptual Art, his films, the Ethics Index, and the body of writing he left on his own mental illness and childhood are all considered active cultural materials. The Index continues to be a required reporting metric in seventy-three national markets as of this writing. CoCA continues to grow. The films continue to play.
