Why this page exists

A living document

This is a compendium of guidance — a chronicle, kept and added to over time, of what Daisaku Ikeda taught, and of the teachers who stood behind him: Josei Toda, Tsunesaburo Makiguchi, and, at the source, the thirteenth-century Japanese priest Nichiren.

It is built on one rule. Every quotation here is a real quotation, taken from a published source and confirmed against the printed page by a named person. Nothing is paraphrased into someone's mouth. Where a line of Ikeda's rests on something Toda said, or Makiguchi wrote, or Nichiren established, the footnote follows it down to that root.

No single reader, and certainly no machine, can do this alone. The page is built in the open so that it can be checked in the open. What you see below is honest about its own state at every moment: each entry shows whether it has been verified, and by whom.

How it is built

Proposed, verified, disputed

Every entry in the chronicle carries one of three marks. The mark is the entry's honest status — not a decoration.

Proposed Located and drafted, with a citation, but not yet checked by a human against the printed source. Treat as unconfirmed.
Verified Confirmed against the physical or official source by a named checker, who recorded the page. Trustworthy.
Disputed A checker has flagged the wording, citation, or attribution. Stays visible, marked, until resolved.

The workflow is deliberate, and it is slow on purpose:

  1. 1A batch of quotations is drafted from the active source and marked Proposed.
  2. 2The quotations are reviewed for fit and framing.
  3. 3Checkers confirm each quotation against the book in hand — exact wording, exact page. Not from memory. Memory is how paraphrase slips in.
  4. 4Confirmed entries become Verified, with the checker's name and date. Flagged entries become Disputed until resolved.
  5. 5The next batch begins. One source is completed before the next is opened.

First, the ground it all stands on

The Lotus Sutra, plainly

Nearly everything on this page traces back to a single Buddhist text: the Lotus Sutra. It is roughly two thousand years old, was first written in Sanskrit, and runs to twenty-eight chapters. People cite it constantly and explain it rarely. Here is what it actually says, without the incense.

The one claim that matters

Earlier Buddhist teaching, in broad strokes, sorted people into tiers. Some could reach enlightenment; some could only get partway; the fully awakened state of a Buddha was treated as something rare, reserved, and far off. The Lotus Sutra walks in and says: no. Every single living being, without exception, already has the capacity for Buddhahood — full enlightenment — right now. Not after countless lifetimes of climbing. Not for monks only. Not for men only. Everyone, as they are.

That is the whole revolution. The sutra calls it the One Vehicle: the idea that the many separate paths people were sold are really one path, open to all, leading to the same place.

The second claim: the Buddha never left

Halfway through, the text does something stranger. The historical Buddha — the man who lived and died in India — reveals that his lifespan is, in effect, unbounded: that the awakened life is not a person who came and went but a reality always present and available. Put plainly: enlightenment is not a locked room someone once stood in. It is a fact about life itself, here, continuously.

What you are supposed to do about it

The sutra does not ask for retreat from the world. It asks the opposite. The people it most exalts are the ones who carry the teaching into ordinary life and ordinary society, who keep going under pressure, who treat every person they meet as someone already carrying that same hidden Buddhahood. The work is not to escape the world. The work is to wake up inside it, and to help others do the same.

Why Nichiren built everything on it

Twelve centuries later, in Japan, Nichiren concluded that this sutra was the heart of all Buddhism, and that its promise had to be made practical — available to a farmer, a fisherman, anyone, without scholarship or privilege. He taught that one could connect to the entire truth of the Lotus Sutra through a single devotional phrase, Nam-myoho-renge-kyo, understood as the Mystic Law: the fundamental law of life that the sutra points to. Chanting it is the act of tuning a life to that law.

So when this page quotes Ikeda on human dignity, on the worth of one person, on never writing anyone off — that is not motivational language. It is the Lotus Sutra's one claim, restated for the present: everyone, already, without exception.

On this summary. The section above is a plain-language introduction, not a quotation, and is offered as an entry point rather than a scholarly account. Corrections from those with deeper training are welcomed — see Help check it.

Key figures

The people inside the teaching

The teachings on this page are populated by figures — some historical, some from within the Lotus Sutra itself — who carry a specific meaning. This section introduces them, one at a time. It begins with the figure who most directly embodies what the chronicle is about.

Bodhisattva Never Disparaging

Never Disparaging (Sanskrit: Sadaparibhuta) is a bodhisattva who appears in the twentieth chapter of the Lotus Sutra. His practice was a single act, repeated for a lifetime. He would bow to every person he met — everyone, without exception — and say, in effect: I deeply respect you. You will become a Buddha.

People mocked him for it. Some cursed him; some struck him; some threw stones. He withdrew to a distance when attacked, but he did not stop, and he did not change the words. He bowed to the people who threw the stones too. The sutra gives him his name from the sentence he would not stop saying.

The point of the figure is not gentleness for its own sake. It is that Never Disparaging saw something that was actually there — the Buddha nature in every person he bowed to — and refused to let other people's behavior, or his own treatment, talk him out of seeing it. Nichiren identified closely with this bodhisattva and read his own persecutions through that chapter. He treated the practice of Never Disparaging as the essential behavior of a Buddhist: not a doctrine to be held, but a way of treating the person in front of you.

This figure stands at the head of the section because the chronicle's recurring theme — the worth of one person, the person right in front of you, happiness that is not pursued alone — is, in the end, Never Disparaging's bow, restated.

This section grows. Further figures — Shakyamuni, T'ien-t'ai, the Bodhisattvas of the Earth, Shijo Kingo and the named disciples of Nichiren — will be added here, each introduced plainly, each cross-linked to the chronicle entries and glossary terms that depend on them.
Glossary

The terms, in plain words

Nichiren Buddhism and the SGI carry a working vocabulary. The definitions below are plain-language entry points, not technical or doctrinal statements. They are held to the same correction standard as the rest of the page — see Help check it.

Nam-myoho-renge-kyo

The invocation at the center of the practice, which Nichiren established. It is understood as the name of the Mystic Law — the fundamental law of life and the universe that the Lotus Sutra points to. To chant it is to align one's own life with that law. The phrase is often translated, loosely, as devotion (nam) to the Mystic Law (myoho) of cause and effect (renge) through sound or teaching (kyo).

The Mystic Law

The English term for the fundamental law named by Nam-myoho-renge-kyo. “Mystic” here does not mean vague; it means the law is profound and not graspable by reasoning alone. Nichiren writes that myo — the “mystic” element — carries the sense of “to revive, to return to life.”

Human revolution

The SGI's term for inner transformation: a fundamental change in a single person's life — in character, in state of life — achieved through Buddhist practice. The conviction is that this inner change in one person is the true and durable starting point of any change in society. It is the title concept of Ikeda's central work and the engine beneath most of the chronicle.

The Ten Worlds — and the “three thousand realms”

Buddhism describes ten basic life-conditions, or Ten Worlds, that a person moves through — from states of suffering and rage, through calm and rapture, up to the awakened state of Buddhahood. The crucial teaching is that all ten are present in each person at every moment: a life in despair still contains Buddhahood, and a life in Buddhahood still contains the rest. One does not climb a ladder and leave the lower rungs behind; one shifts which world is manifest.

From this comes the formulation often rendered as the three thousand realms in a single moment of life (Japanese: ichinen sanzen). The “three thousand” is the result of a multiplication — the ten worlds, each containing all ten, multiplied again across ten factors and three realms of existence — and the number itself is not the point. The point the number is making is the point: the whole of life, in all its possible conditions, is fully present in one ordinary moment of one ordinary person's experience. Nothing has to be reached for. Everything is already here, now, to be transformed.

Kosen-rufu

A term of Nichiren's, naming the wide spread of the Lotus Sutra's teaching through society — and, in the SGI's reading, its application toward a peaceful and humane world. It is the concept Pissarro identified as the origin of his own thinking. See Entry 001.

Absolute happiness

A distinction Ikeda attributes to second president Josei Toda. Relative happiness depends on circumstance and on comparison with others, and fades when conditions change. Absolute happiness is a state of life in which existence itself is felt as joy, regardless of circumstance. The practice aims at the second. See Entry 002.

Gohonzon

The object of devotion in Nichiren Buddhism — a scroll inscribed by Nichiren, embodying the Mystic Law, before which practitioners chant. Toda described it, plainly, as a “happiness-producing device.”

The Bodhisattvas of the Earth

In the Lotus Sutra, a vast host of bodhisattvas who emerge from beneath the earth and are entrusted with carrying the teaching into the troubled future age. The SGI understands its own members as taking up exactly that role. A fuller entry will follow in Key figures.

WND — The Writings of Nichiren Daishonin

The standard English edition of Nichiren's letters and treatises, in two volumes. A citation such as WND-1, 471 means volume 1, page 471. “Daishonin,” meaning “great sage,” is the honorific by which Nichiren is referred to within the tradition. See Entry 006.

This glossary grows. Terms are added as the chronicle and the Key figures section require them. Each definition is a plain entry point and welcomes correction from those with deeper training.
The lineage

Four names, one thread

Every quotation in the chronicle is read against the line of teachers it descends from. The thread runs in one direction, and the page always footnotes back along it.

Nichiren 1222–1282 → Makiguchi 1871–1944 → Toda 1900–1958 → Ikeda 1928–2023

Nichiren — the root

Thirteenth-century Japanese Buddhist priest. Held that the Lotus Sutra was the complete and final teaching, and that its promise — Buddhahood for everyone, now — had to be made usable by ordinary people through the practice of Nam-myoho-renge-kyo. He was exiled twice and nearly executed for saying so. Everything below descends from him.

Tsunesaburo Makiguchi — the first president

Educator, geographer, and philosopher. Built a theory of value creation — that the purpose of education, and of a life, is the creation of value, and that value's truest name is happiness. In 1930 he founded the organization that would become the Soka Gakkai. Imprisoned as a “thought criminal” by Japan's wartime government for refusing the state religion, he died in prison in 1944 without recanting.

Josei Toda — the second president

Makiguchi's disciple, imprisoned alongside him. He survived prison, walked out, and rebuilt the organization from almost nothing into a movement of hundreds of thousands. Toda gave the practice its modern shape and its momentum.

Daisaku Ikeda — the third president, “Sensei”

Toda's disciple. Took the movement global, founding the Soka Gakkai International in 1975, and devoted his life to peace, education, and dialogue across cultures. A Buddhist philosopher, author, and poet. It is his guidance this page chronicles — and the honorific “Sensei,” teacher, is the one his students use for him.

The four capsule descriptions above are summary biography, not quotation. They are held to the same correction standard as the rest of the page.
Help check it

This cannot be built alone

Who this asks for

This page needs checkers: people who own these books, study them, and will confirm a quotation by holding the printed source and reading the page — not by recognizing it from memory. Recognizing a quote from memory feels like verification and is not; it is how a paraphrase becomes mistaken for a quotation. A checker confirms exact wording, exact citation, exact page.

What a check involves

Take any entry marked Proposed. Find the cited passage in the book. Confirm the words match exactly, the citation is right, and the attribution — including any speech date or occasion — is correct. Then write in with what you found, and your name, so the entry can be marked Verified or Disputed.

How to write in
